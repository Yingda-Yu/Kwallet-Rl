\begin{abstract}
Streaming payment channels must route every arriving transaction into one of
$k$ collateral wallets or drop it, periodically flushing a wallet at a fixed
fee. We study the resulting collateral-control problem as an online,
partially-specified Markov decision process under twelve traffic regimes.
We (i) faithfully port the environment/traffic generator and re-implement three
PPO policies---a joint action head, independent factorized heads, and a
settle-conditioned factored head. Across five seeds, the settle-conditioned
factored head significantly beats the joint head at all four tested
capacities, and the independently factored head at three of four, whereas the
extra settle-conditioning path gives \emph{no statistically detectable} gain
(paired 95\% CIs; zero- and shuffled-conditioning ablations are
indistinguishable), a negative mechanism result we report. (ii) We introduce a
permutation-equivariant set encoder with no $k$-dependent parameter, which
loads and runs zero-shot across wallet counts where a flat MLP cannot; its
transfer quality is asymmetric, and at the largest tested $k$ it trails a
size-matched flat network.
(iii) A deliberately strong, validation-selected threshold rule attains the
highest Money and significantly outperforms the learned policies in nearly all
paired comparisons on stationary streams, and incurs zero avoidable drops
after abrupt regime shifts. On this balanced workload, wallet
fill is close to a sufficient statistic; we keep this negative rather than hide
it. All numbers are produced by released, deterministic code on ported data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Off-chain payment networks such as the Lightning Network~\cite{poon2016lightning}
process high-rate transaction streams over wallets backed by finite collateral.
A controller observes the current transaction and wallet state, then either
settles it into a wallet with sufficient free balance or drops it; it may also
flush (empty and recharge) one wallet, incurring a fee and a short cooldown.
Collateral $C$ is partitioned into $k$ wallets, so capacity is per-wallet, not
shared: the controller must decide \emph{where} to settle and \emph{when/what}
to flush under non-stationary, bursty traffic. Prior control schemes use fixed
heuristics; recent work proposes learning the controller with policy
optimization over a structured action space.

We treat that line as a hypothesis to audit rather than a result to defend:
\emph{which structural inductive biases actually matter for online collateral
control---a joint versus factored action head, a settle-conditioned flush
head, a $k$-invariant encoder---and does learning beat a strong, cheap
feedback rule?} Re-implementing the environment and policies, we benchmark
against a validation-tuned rule (not only naive heuristics), add mechanism
ablations, and redesign the encoder for the deployment reality that the number
of wallets can change. The environment and twelve-regime generator are a
faithful port of the prior manuscript; JA-PPO/IFAC/SC-FAC and their
hyperparameters are re-implementations (the manuscript omits them); the strong
rule, the conditioning ablations, and the equivariant set encoder are this
study's additions. Our findings are deliberately reported whether positive or
null:
\begin{itemize}
\item A faithful, tested re-implementation of the environment and the
twelve-regime traffic generator, and of three PPO policies---\textbf{JA-PPO}
(joint action over $(k{+}1)^2$ outcomes), \textbf{IFAC} (independent settle and
flush heads, $2(k{+}1)$ outputs), and \textbf{SC-FAC} (flush head conditioned
on the chosen settlement wallet). We report the environment as a faithful port
and the learned policies/hyperparameters as re-implementations.
\item A \textbf{permutation-equivariant set encoder} (shared per-wallet
function + pooling + per-wallet logits, in the spirit of Deep
Sets~\cite{zaheer2017deepsets}) with no parameter that depends on $k$, which
transfers \emph{zero-shot} across wallet counts; a flat multilayer perceptron
cannot (its weight shapes change with $k$).
\item \textbf{Mechanism ablations} that zero or shuffle the conditioning
signal over matched seeds; they find no detectable Money benefit from the
settle$\to$flush path at the tested flush price---an honest null result.
\item An \textbf{honest strong baseline}: a validation-selected best-fit
threshold rule that attains zero avoidable drops, is robust to regime shifts,
and attains the highest Money at every capacity, significantly beating the
learned policies in nearly all paired comparisons. We report where learning
does---and, more often, does not---beat it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Streaming collateral control}
\textbf{Model.} Collateral $C\in\{800,900,1000,1200\}$ is split into $k=24$
wallets of capacity $C/k$. At each of $T=1000$ decisions a transaction of size
$x_t\in[0,1000]$ arrives. The agent picks a settle wallet $a_s\in[0,k]$ and a
flush wallet $a_f\in[0,k]$, where index $k$ is a null (no-op). A flush executes
first, emptying a wallet and making it unavailable for $F{=}3$ decisions before
it refills to full; then the transaction is accepted if $a_s$ is usable and has
free balance $\ge x_t$. Drops are attributed as \emph{oversize} ($x_t>C/k$,
physically impossible), \emph{frozen} (cooldown), or \emph{avoidable}
(insufficient/conflict that a proactive flush could have prevented). The
observation is $3k{+}2$: per-wallet normalized balance, availability, and
cooldown, plus $x_t$ normalized and time progress.

\textbf{Objective.} Money is $M=p\cdot\sum\!\text{accepted}_x-\tau\cdot\#\text{flushes}$
with $p=1,\tau=10$; episodic PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} with GAE
advantages~\cite{schulman2015gae} optimizes the policy. \textbf{Data.}
Episodes are drawn from twelve regimes spanning steady/large transactions and
burst traffic (calibrated to a target mean), with fixed seeds; 5000 training,
300 validation, and 200 test episodes per regime. All hyperparameters not given
by the prior manuscript are fixed from validation pilots, never from test.

\section{Policies}
\noindent\textbf{JA-PPO} emits a categorical over all $(a_s,a_f)$ pairs,
i.e.\ $(k{+}1)^2{=}625$ logits at $k{=}24$, conflating the two decisions.
\textbf{IFAC} factors the action into two independent $k{+}1$ categoricals
(50 logits). \textbf{SC-FAC} factors but routes an embedding of the \emph{chosen}
settle wallet into the flush head, so flushing can depend on where value was
just placed. Two ablations share its architecture: \textbf{no-cond.} zeroes the
settle embedding, and \textbf{shuffled} feeds a deranged (wrong) wallet
embedding.

\noindent\textbf{Set encoder.} The $k$ wallets are an unordered set, yet a flat
MLP treats wallet slots as a fixed-length ordered vector and ties its weights
to $k$. We encode each wallet with a shared function
$h_i=\phi(\text{balance}_i,\text{avail}_i,\text{cooldown}_i,g)$ of the wallet
state and two global scalars, pool $\{h_i\}$, and produce per-wallet settle and
flush logits. The map is equivariant to wallet permutations (proven by tests),
and no layer's size depends on $k$, so weights trained at one $k$ load and run
at another. The settle-conditioned set variant gathers the feature of the
chosen settle wallet into the flush head.

\section{Experiments}
We evaluate deterministic policies on the fixed test pools. Model and
training choices are made on validation only; we keep failed seeds and report
negatives. \emph{Baselines} are the native one-flush references FA and FWF
(reconstructed, since the reference uses a multi-flush design), a rotation
rule, and the strong \textbf{BFP0.5}: best-fit routing that proactively flushes
the fullest usable wallet once its fill crosses a threshold $0.5$, chosen on
validation. We also construct an oracle feasible value (transactions that fit
some wallet).

\noindent\textbf{Stationary performance.} Table~\ref{tab:main} reports Money per
capacity with seed-level paired contrasts in Table~\ref{tab:paired}; the
structural floor of oversize drops is $\approx400$ per episode for every method.
Two robust effects and one null emerge. \emph{Factorization helps:}
SC-FAC significantly outperforms JA-PPO at all four capacities
($p\!\le\!0.013$, $5/5$ seeds); IFAC also outperforms JA-PPO at
$C\!\in\!\{800,900,1200\}$ ($p\!\le\!0.033$) but \emph{not} at $C{=}1000$
($p{=}0.57$). \emph{Conditioning does not add Money:} the SC-FAC$-$IFAC paired
difference has a 95\% CI including zero at $C\!\in\!\{900,1000,1200\}$, and at
$C{=}800$ SC-FAC is significantly \emph{lower} than IFAC ($-419$, $p{=}0.009$);
SC-FAC accepts marginally more value but spends more flushes, which cancels the
gain at $\tau{=}10$. \emph{A tuned rule beats learning:} BFP0.5 attains the
highest Money at every capacity with zero avoidable drops, and its paired
advantage is significant in $11/12$ method-capacity contrasts; the sole
exception is vs.\ IFAC at $C{=}1000$ ($+2545$, $p{=}0.057$, CI crossing zero),
which BFP0.5 nevertheless wins on all five seeds. On this balanced i.i.d.\
workload a single balance-feedback threshold is near-optimal, and a
3000-episode PPO budget does not beat it.

\noindent\textbf{Cross-$k$ transfer.} We train at $k\in\{6,12,24\}$ and deploy
at every $k$. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} and Fig.~\ref{fig:heat} show that the set
encoder loads and runs at every deployment $k$ with no retraining, whereas flat
policies have no off-diagonal entry because their state-dictionary shapes
change with $k$. The benefit is operational deployability (train once, deploy at
another scale), not uniformly preserved Money: zero-shot retention is
asymmetric---strong when moving to larger-wallet/smaller-$k$ operating points
and weak when a model trained with large wallets meets the small wallets at
$k{=}24$. At matched $k$ the set encoder is comparable to or above a flat MLP
at $k{=}6,12$ but lower at $k{=}24$ ($9190$ vs.\ $13610$), so deployability
comes with a scale-specific accuracy trade-off.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
On balanced stationary streams a carefully tuned, extremely simple feedback
rule attains the highest Money and beats the general-purpose PPO controllers in
nearly all paired comparisons; the defensible claims for learning are therefore
structural rather than raw Money. The robust learned effect is
\emph{factorization}: the settle-conditioned factored head beats the joint head
at every capacity and the independent factored head at three of four; the
further settle-conditioning mechanism is a null under our ablation protocol
and should not be presented as a gain. The most useful
systems result is the equivariant set encoder's $k$-independence and
zero-shot cross-$k$ deployability, although we show this is asymmetric and, at
$k{=}24$, sacrifices matched-scale accuracy. We read the strong rule not as a
failure of RL in general but as evidence that, with the present observation
model, wallet fill is close to a sufficient statistic on i.i.d.\ streams;
learning is most likely to matter only under richer structure (non-i.i.d.\
predictable bursts, heterogeneous fees/wallets), which we leave to future work.
Limitations: one flush per step, finite horizon, a fixed fee, no lookahead to
future arrivals, and single-GPU/CPU PPO with no vectorized rollouts. The
prior manuscript's learned numbers are treated as reported, not regenerated:
its environment is a faithful port while the policies and unspecified
hyperparameters are documented re-implementations chosen on validation.

\section{Conclusion}
We audited which structural inductive biases help a learned streaming
collateral controller. The evidence is clear on three points and null on one.
Factoring the joint action into separate settle/flush heads gives a
reproducible gain over the joint head---significant at all four capacities for
the settle-conditioned variant and at three of four for the independent
variant---and cuts outputs from $(k{+}1)^2$ to $2(k{+}1)$. Adding a
settle-conditioned flush path yields no detectable Money gain under
matched-seed zero/shuffle ablations. A $k$-independent equivariant encoder
adds genuine operational value---weights deploy zero-shot to unseen wallet
counts where a flat MLP cannot---at an asymmetric transfer and matched-scale
cost. Most importantly, on homogeneous stationary streams and across abrupt
regime switches, one validation-selected feedback rule attains the highest
Money with zero post-switch avoidable drops and beats the learned controllers
in nearly every paired comparison; we present this as a target for future
learning methods rather than conceal it.
